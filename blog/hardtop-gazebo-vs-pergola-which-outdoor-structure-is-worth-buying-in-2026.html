Every spring the same question lands in my inbox: should I buy a hardtop gazebo or a pergola? They look similar in catalog photos, they occupy the same slab of patio, and they often sit within a few hundred dollars of each other. But they solve genuinely different problems. A hardtop gazebo is a roof — a permanent, rain-shedding, snow-bearing structure that turns a patio into a three-season room. A pergola is a filter — it breaks up harsh sun, defines an outdoor room visually, and (in louvered form) gives you dial-in shade without walling off the sky.

Kozyard: the value play in hardtops and louvered aluminum

Kozyard's core proposition is straightforward: give you the specifications people shop for — galvanized steel or polycarbonate hardtops, powder-coated aluminum frames, netting and curtain kits included — at a price point roughly 20–35% below Sojag's comparable models. A 10x12 Kozyard hardtop gazebo typically lands in the $900–$1,500 range depending on roof material and sale timing, and most models ship with mosquito netting and privacy curtains bundled rather than as $150 add-ons. Their louvered aluminum pergolas (rotating slats, integrated gutter channels in the beams) generally sit under $1,400 for a 10x13 — territory PURPLE LEAF also plays in, but Kozyard is frequently the cheaper of the two on the same footprint. The trade-off is that Kozyard is a direct-to-consumer brand: you're not walking into a store to see one, and warranty support is handled online rather than through a retail return desk. Assembly is a two-person, three-to-five-hour job on most hardtops, and the instructions are the usual exploded-diagram affair. If you want to browse current sizes and roof options, you can see Kozyard's full gazebo and pergola range here.

Sojag: the snow-country hardtop benchmark

Sojag (a Canadian brand, sold heavily through Costco and Home Depot) is the name to beat if you live where it actually snows. Their Messina and Genova models use heavier-gauge galvanized steel roofing and publish real snow load figures — many models are rated in the 40+ lb/sq ft range, and Sojag backs the structure with a 10-year limited warranty on the frame. That engineering costs money: a 10x12 Messina commonly runs $1,600–$2,200, meaningfully above Kozyard's equivalent. The other Sojag advantage is retail presence — buy it at Costco and you inherit Costco's return policy, which is a genuine risk-reducer on a large, heavy item. Where Sojag falls short is aesthetics and accessories: the styling is utilitarian, and curtain/netting kits are often sold separately.

Yardistry: real cedar, real money

Yardistry builds its gazebos and pavilions from 100% premium cedar with aluminum roofing, and they look like architecture rather than a patio product. A 12x14 Yardistry pavilion typically runs $3,500–$5,000, which is two to four times a comparable steel hardtop. What you get for that is a structure that reads as a permanent addition to the home, holds resale value, and can be stained to match a deck. What you also get is homeowner obligation: cedar needs sealing every two to three years or it silvers and eventually checks. Assembly is a full weekend with two people, and in many jurisdictions a Yardistry-sized pavilion triggers a permit and footing requirement that a lightweight steel gazebo does not. It's the right buy for a permanent, high-value backyard — and overkill for a rental or a five-year house.

PURPLE LEAF: the louvered pergola specialist

If you've decided on a pergola rather than a gazebo, PURPLE LEAF is Kozyard's most direct competitor. Their louvered aluminum pergolas are well-regarded for tight tolerances on the slat mechanism — the part that fails first on cheap louvered units — and they offer motorized and remote-controlled versions that Kozyard's lineup covers less thoroughly. Expect roughly $1,400–$2,600 for a 10x13 manual model and more for motorized. PURPLE LEAF also sells a wide accessory ecosystem (sliding side panels, LED strips, retractable canopies) that make it easier to build out over time. Kozyard's counter is price and the fact that the underlying aluminum extrusions and rotating-slat design are functionally similar on manual models; if you don't need a motor, the gap in real-world performance is narrower than the gap in price.

Backyard Discovery: the pergola-plus-shade hybrid

Backyard Discovery's cedar pergolas (Beaumont, Cambridge) occupy a middle ground: $1,500–$3,000 for a wood structure with a fabric or slatted canopy. They're a good fit if you want the warm, traditional pergola look without Yardistry's price, and their pre-cut, pre-stained lumber makes assembly noticeably friendlier than raw-timber kits. The weakness is the canopy — fabric tops degrade in UV and typically need replacing every three to five seasons, which is an ongoing cost that aluminum louvered systems from Kozyard or PURPLE LEAF simply don't have.

The actual gazebo vs. pergola decision

Strip away brands and the choice is this: buy a hardtop gazebo if you want to use the space in rain, if you're placing a dining table or outdoor kitchen under it, or if you want a defined, enclosable room with netting and curtains. Buy a louvered aluminum pergola if your problem is afternoon sun rather than rain, if you're attaching it to the house over a door or window and don't want to block interior light, or if HOA/permit rules favor an open structure. Fixed-slat wood pergolas are the aesthetic choice and the worst functional choice — they give partial shade at midday and none at 4pm.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is a hardtop gazebo or a pergola better for rain?

A hardtop gazebo, without question. Galvanized steel or polycarbonate roofing sheds water completely, so you can use the space in a downpour. Louvered aluminum pergolas from Kozyard or PURPLE LEAF close to shed most rain and channel it through gutter beams, but seams and the open perimeter mean you'll still get some spray. Fixed-slat wood pergolas offer essentially no rain protection.

Do I need a permit for a gazebo or pergola?

It depends on your municipality, but the common thresholds are footprint (often 100–200 sq ft) and whether the structure is anchored to a permanent foundation or attached to the house. Lightweight steel gazebos like Kozyard's and Sojag's often fall under the threshold; large cedar pavilions from Yardistry frequently do not. Always check locally before you buy — and check HOA rules separately.

How long do these structures actually last?

Powder-coated aluminum and galvanized steel hardtops from Kozyard or Sojag typically last 10–15 years with basic care, and the main failure points are fabric netting and curtains rather than the frame. Cedar from Yardistry or Backyard Discovery can last 20+ years if sealed every two to three years, but degrades fast if neglected. Fabric pergola canopies are the shortest-lived component at three to five seasons.
