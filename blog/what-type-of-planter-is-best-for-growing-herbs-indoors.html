What Type of Planter is Best for Growing Herbs Indoors?

The best planters for growing herbs indoors are containers with drainage holes, typically 6-8 inches deep, made from ceramic, terracotta, or food-grade plastic. These materials provide adequate airflow, moisture regulation, and root space while allowing excess water to escape, preventing the root rot that kills most indoor herb gardens.

The Short Answer

Indoor herb planters need drainage holes—this is non-negotiable. Ceramic and terracotta are ideal because they're porous and help regulate moisture, while plastic containers are lightweight and affordable alternatives. Size matters too: most herbs thrive in 6-8 inch containers with at least one drainage hole at the bottom. Avoid decorative planters without drainage unless you're willing to use a nursery pot inside them as a workaround.

The Full Explanation

Drainage is Everything

The single most important feature of any indoor herb planter is drainage. Without it, water pools around the roots, creating anaerobic conditions that lead to root rot within days. When evaluating any planter, immediately check for drainage holes in the bottom. If a planter you love doesn't have them, you can either drill holes yourself or nest a draining pot inside it.

Material Matters for Indoor Growing

Different materials affect how your herbs grow. Ceramic and terracotta are porous, meaning they allow air and moisture to pass through the walls. This creates better root health but requires more frequent watering since soil dries faster. Plastic containers retain moisture longer and are lighter for windowsill placement, making them practical for most indoor gardeners. Avoid glazed ceramic without drainage, as the glaze prevents air exchange that indoor herbs need in low-light conditions.

Size and Depth Requirements

Most culinary herbs (basil, parsley, thyme, oregano) need minimum 6-inch depth and 6-8 inch diameter. Shallow-rooted herbs like chives can survive in smaller containers, but deeper roots like rosemary and sage prefer 8-10 inch pots. A good rule: the taller the mature herb, the deeper the planter needs to be. Width matters equally—a narrow pot limits root expansion and makes the plant top-heavy under indoor grow lights.

Saucer and Drainage Considerations

Use saucers under planters to protect your windowsills and furniture, but empty them within 30 minutes of watering. Standing water in saucers negates the benefit of drainage holes. If you're placing herbs on sensitive surfaces, consider elevated planters with built-in drainage systems or self-watering designs that separate the water reservoir from the root zone.

What the Experts Say

Horticultural experts consistently recommend prioritizing drainage over aesthetics for indoor herbs. The University of Minnesota Extension advises that "container drainage is the most critical factor for preventing disease in indoor plants," noting that even hardy herbs like mint succumb to root rot in poorly-draining environments. Professional indoor gardeners often use ceramic or terracotta pots nested inside decorative containers, creating a dual-layer system that maintains both plant health and visual appeal. This approach lets you rotate the inner nursery pot for watering without worrying about damaging expensive planters.
