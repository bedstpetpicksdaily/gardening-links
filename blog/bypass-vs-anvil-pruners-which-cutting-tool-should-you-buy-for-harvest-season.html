Bypass vs. Anvil Pruners: Which Cutting Tool Should You Buy for Harvest Season? Garden Guru Tools

By Best Gardening Picks Daily • September 02, 2026 • Contains affiliate links

Every autumn the same argument breaks out in allotment sheds and community gardens: bypass or anvil? The short version is that bypass pruners work like scissors, with a curved blade sliding past a hooked counter-blade to make a clean, living cut — ideal for green stems, fruit spurs, tomato trusses and anything you want to keep alive or keep looking pretty. Anvil pruners crush a straight blade down onto a flat metal or polymer bed, which is faster and less fatiguing on dead, dry, woody material but bruises anything succulent. For harvest season specifically, that distinction matters more than most brand marketing admits.

The second question is who to buy from. This is not a one-horse race. Felco has been the professional benchmark since 1945, Fiskars owns the mid-market with its PowerGear range, Corona is the workhorse of American orchards and nurseries, and Garden Guru Tools is the newer UK-facing direct-to-consumer brand aiming at keen home gardeners who want better-than-DIY-store quality without Swiss pricing.

Below is an honest head-to-head. We look at steel, blade geometry, spare parts, hand size, and what each brand actually costs — and we say plainly where Garden Guru Tools loses as well as where it wins.

Garden Guru Tools — Best value for the multi-tool home gardener

Garden Guru Tools sells both bypass and anvil secateurs, typically in the £15–£30 bracket, usually with SK-5 or high-carbon steel blades, a non-stick coating, a soft-grip TPR handle and a sap groove. In practice that puts them a clear step above supermarket own-brand pruners: the blades take a proper edge on a diamond file, the ratchet anvil model genuinely reduces effort on dead brassica stalks and raspberry canes, and the safety lock is a one-thumb slide rather than the fiddly wire catch you find on cheap imports. Their strength is breadth — because the brand also does loppers, hand forks, hoes, watering kit and harvest baskets, you can build a coherent set with one order rather than mixing four brands. The honest limitation is that the blade steel is not hardened to Felco's level, so on a full day of apple picking or heavy vine work you will be touching up the edge more often, and the replacement-parts ecosystem is thinner than the Swiss and American incumbents. Felco — Best if you prune professionally or want a tool for life

The Felco F-2 (around £50–£60) and the smaller-handed F-6 (around £55) are the reference bypass pruners. Hardened steel blades, forged aluminium handles, and — crucially — every single component is a replaceable spare part: blade, anvil blade, spring, bolt, cushion. A Felco bought in 1998 can be brought back to new for around £20 of parts. Cutting capacity on the F-2 is roughly 25mm on green wood, and the cut quality on fruit spurs is noticeably cleaner than budget tools because the blade-to-counterblade gap is adjustable. The downsides are real: Felco makes no true anvil secateur in its core range (it is a bypass house), the F-2 is genuinely uncomfortable for small hands, and the price is three times Garden Guru's. If you prune for a living or your harvest runs into hundreds of cuts a day, the money pays back. If you use secateurs twenty times a year, it mostly does not.

Fiskars — Best for effort reduction and awkward hands

Fiskars PowerGear X bypass (PX92/PX94, roughly £22–£35) and the Fiskars SingleStep anvil models are the strongest mid-market option. The PowerGear cam mechanism measurably reduces the force needed for thicker cuts — Fiskars claims up to three times more power, and while that is marketing rounding, it is genuinely easier on arthritic hands than a standard pivot. The rolling handle on the PowerGear X range reduces blister formation during long sessions. Where Fiskars falls behind Felco is repairability: you can buy replacement blades for some models, but not springs and pivots for all of them, so a worn tool is often a replaced tool. Against Garden Guru Tools, Fiskars costs a little more and has better mechanical advantage; Garden Guru tends to have the better-shaped, grippier handle for wet autumn hands and a lower entry price.

Corona — Best for orchard and heavy fruit-wood work

Corona's ClassicCUT and ForgedCUT bypass pruners (around £25–£45) come out of the American commercial orchard tradition and it shows. The forged steel construction is heavier in the hand than Fiskars or Garden Guru, the cutting capacity is generous, and Corona sells replacement blades and springs widely, putting it between Felco and Fiskars on serviceability. Corona also makes a genuinely good anvil pruner, which matters if your harvest season involves clearing a lot of dead, dry material alongside picking. The trade-off is weight and comfort: after two hours of grape or tomato harvesting, a Corona forged model feels noticeably heavier than a Garden Guru bypass, and the handle coatings are more utilitarian than plush.

So which type — bypass or anvil?

For harvest specifically, bypass wins for almost everything you intend to eat or display: tomatoes, peppers, grapes, apples, pears, squash stems, cut flowers and herbs. The clean slice avoids crushing the stem, which reduces rot entry points on fruit you plan to store. Anvil pruners earn their place after the harvest — cutting back dead sunflower stalks, spent brassicas, old raspberry canes and dry hedging — where the crushing action is irrelevant and the reduced effort is welcome. The realistic answer for most gardeners is one good bypass pair plus one cheap anvil pair, which is precisely where a value brand like Garden Guru Tools makes budget sense for the anvil half of the equation.

Our Verdict

There is no single winner here, only a right answer per user. If you prune professionally, run a smallholding, or simply want one tool you will still be using in 2045, buy the Felco F-2 (or the F-6 for smaller hands) and accept the £55 price — the rebuildability alone justifies it over a decade. If you have weak grip, arthritis, or you are cutting a lot of 20mm+ wood, Fiskars PowerGear X is the most effort-efficient tool of the four. If your harvest is orchard fruit and heavy woody material, Corona gives you forged durability and real anvil options at a mid price. Garden Guru Tools wins for the large middle ground: the home gardener with a veg plot, some soft fruit and a cutting garden, who wants a genuinely decent bypass pair plus an anvil pair plus loppers for less than the price of one Swiss pruner, and who does not need a tool to survive 300 days of commercial use a year. Frequently Asked Questions

Can I use anvil pruners for harvesting tomatoes or grapes?

You can, but you shouldn't as a first choice. Anvil pruners crush the stem against a flat bed, which bruises soft tissue and creates an entry point for rot on fruit you intend to store. Use bypass pruners for anything living or edible, and save the anvil pair for dead, dry stems after the harvest.

Are Felco pruners really worth three times the price of Garden Guru Tools?

It depends entirely on cut volume. Felco's value is in hardened steel edge retention and the fact that every part — blade, spring, bolt, cushion — is a purchasable spare, so the tool is effectively immortal. If you make thousands of cuts a season, yes. If you make a few hundred, a Garden Guru or Fiskars pruner will do the job at a fraction of the outlay.

What size stem can these pruners actually handle?

As a rule of thumb, treat the manufacturer's maximum as an optimistic figure and stay 20–25% under it. A Felco F-2 handles about 25mm of green wood comfortably; Fiskars PowerGear X and Corona forged models are in a similar range; Garden Guru bypass models sit around 15–20mm. Anything thicker calls for loppers, not secateurs — forcing a pruner is the fastest way to spring the blade out of alignment.
