Step 1: Deep Clean Everything Before You Store It

Never store dirty furniture. Pollen, tree sap, bird droppings, sunscreen residue, and food spills all become permanent stains after a few months in a dark shed. Start with a mild solution of warm water and dish soap for most surfaces. For aluminum and powder-coated steel frames, a soft cloth and gentle soap is plenty — avoid abrasive pads that scratch protective coatings. Wicker and resin benefit from a soft brush to get into the weave. Teak and hardwoods can be cleaned with a wood-specific cleaner, then left to dry completely before oiling or sealing. Cushions and fabric slings should be spot-treated for mildew with a diluted vinegar or oxygen-bleach solution, rinsed, and — this is the critical part — dried thoroughly on both sides. Even slightly damp cushions will grow mold in storage. Give everything a full 24 to 48 hours of dry time in the sun or a well-ventilated garage.

Step 2: Protect Frames, Fasteners, and Finishes

Once clean and dry, treat each material for what it is. Wood furniture needs a coat of sealant or teak oil to prevent moisture penetration and cracking. Metal frames should be inspected for chips in the finish — touch up bare spots with matching outdoor paint, because a pinhole of exposed steel is where rust starts. Apply a light coat of automotive wax or silicone spray to aluminum and steel frames as a moisture barrier. Tighten every bolt and screw while you're at it; thermal expansion and contraction over winter loosens hardware, and a wobbly chair in spring usually traces back to a bolt you ignored in October. Spray hinges, umbrella mechanisms, and folding joints with a light penetrating lubricant so they don't seize. Finally, flip tables and chairs to check the undersides — that's where rust, spider nests, and hidden damage hide.

Step 3: Store Smart — Or Build a Space That Doesn't Need Storage

The gold standard is indoor storage: a garage, basement, or shed keeps furniture completely out of the elements. Stack chairs where possible, store cushions in breathable bags or bins (never sealed plastic that traps humidity), and keep everything elevated off concrete floors, which wick moisture upward. If indoor space is limited, use quality waterproof covers with vents, secure them with straps or bungees against wind, and elevate furniture on blocks or pallets so water doesn't pool underneath. Instead of hauling everything into a cramped garage, you simply cover and leave it in place — and you keep an usable outdoor room through winter. Step 4: Don't Forget Planters, Screens, and the Rest of the Patio

Winterizing goes beyond chairs and tables. Ceramic and terracotta planters crack when soil freezes and expands — either bring them inside or switch to freeze-resistant resin or metal options. Kozyard's raised garden beds and planters are built from galvanized steel and weather-stable materials that handle freeze-thaw cycles without splitting, which is a genuine relief if you've ever lost a favorite pot to a January cold snap. Empty and drain any planters staying outdoors, and elevate them slightly for drainage. Privacy screens and fence panels should be checked for loose anchors and cleaned of climbing vines that trap moisture. Roll up and secure retractable awnings before the first heavy snow — most awning damage happens when fabric is left extended and accumulates ice weight. And take five minutes to clear gutters and downspouts near your patio so meltwater drains away from your furniture zone rather than pooling around it. A little attention here saves hundreds in spring repairs.

Frequently Asked Questions

When exactly should I winterize my patio furniture?

Aim for the period after your last regular use but before the first hard frost — typically mid-to-late October in most northern climates, and November in milder zones. Watch the forecast for the first sustained overnight temperatures below freezing and give yourself a dry weekend a week or two ahead of that.

Can patio furniture stay outside under a gazebo all winter?

Yes, in most cases. A hardtop gazebo or pergola with a solid roof blocks direct snow and rain, which handles the biggest threats. For best results, still cover cushions and store fabric items indoors, and use breathable furniture covers underneath the structure. Kozyard's hardtop gazebos are built with snow-rated steel and polycarbonate roofing specifically for this kind of year-round protection.

Are waterproof covers enough on their own, or do I need indoor storage?

Quality vented covers work well for metal and resin furniture, but they're only effective if the furniture is clean and completely dry first, and if the cover is strapped down against wind. Cushions, wood pieces, and anything with electronics or fabric slings do better indoors. Never use non-breathable plastic tarps — they trap condensation and cause more mildew than they prevent.
