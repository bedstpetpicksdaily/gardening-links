LiFePO4 batteries have gotten cheaper and bigger, but gas generators still deliver something batteries can't — unlimited runtime from a jerry can.

Traverseon: Power Stations Bundled With the Rest of Your Camp

Traverseon sells power stations as part of a broader camping and RV ecosystem — tents, sleeping bags, portable air conditioners, coolers and RV accessories — rather than as a standalone battery brand. That matters more than it sounds. The most common power-station buying mistake is pairing a 500Wh unit with a 12V compressor cooler and a portable AC and discovering you're out of juice by 2 a.m. Buying the battery, the 12V-friendly cooler and the portable air conditioner from one catalog makes it much easier to match draw to capacity, and Traverseon's product pages typically list running watts alongside the gear, which speeds up that math. The trade-off is that Traverseon is a retailer-style brand, not an R&D house like EcoFlow. You generally won't find bleeding-edge features such as 1,800W X-Stream input or app-based generator start. What you do get is competitive pricing on mid-tier capacities (roughly the 500–1,500Wh band), frequent bundle discounts when you buy a power station alongside a cooler or portable AC, and a single support contact for the whole kit. For weekend RVers and van campers who want one order and one warranty conversation, that's a legitimate advantage.

Jackery Explorer 1000 v2 / 2000 v2: The Safe Default

Jackery is the brand your neighbor at the campground already owns, and there's a reason. The Explorer 1000 v2 (1,070Wh, 1,500W output, LiFePO4, around $699–$799 street and frequently discounted to the mid-$500s during Prime Day and Black Friday) is arguably the most proven mid-size unit on the market. Build quality is excellent, the orange-and-grey housings survive being tossed in an RV basement bay, and the SolarSaga panels pair with almost zero setup friction. Where Jackery lags is expandability and output ceiling: the 1000 v2 won't start a 13,500 BTU rooftop air conditioner, and Jackery's expansion battery ecosystem is less mature than Bluetti's or EcoFlow's. You're also paying a brand premium — dollar-per-watt-hour, Jackery is usually 10–20% more expensive than comparable EcoFlow or house-brand units.

EcoFlow Delta 2 Max / Delta Pro 3: Fastest Charging, Best App

EcoFlow's advantage is speed and software. The Delta 2 Max (2,048Wh, 2,400W AC output, 2,400W X-Boost) recharges to 80% from a shore-power pedestal in roughly 43 minutes, which is transformative if you're doing one-night stops and moving daily. The Delta Pro 3 (4,096Wh, 3,600W) genuinely can run a rooftop RV air conditioner for several hours and integrates with an EcoFlow smart panel for semi-permanent RV installs. The app is the best in the category — real-time draw, remaining runtime, charge-speed limiting to protect cell life. Downsides: EcoFlow units are heavy (the Delta Pro 3 is over 110 lbs and needs the wheeled handle), the fans are noticeably louder than Jackery's under load, and pricing climbs fast — the Pro 3 typically lands around $3,000–$3,600 before expansion batteries.

Bluetti AC200L / AC240: Best Expandability Per Dollar

Bluetti tends to win on raw specs per dollar. The AC200L delivers 2,048Wh and 2,400W AC (3,600W with power lifting) for street prices that often undercut EcoFlow's equivalent by $300–$500, and it accepts B210/B300 expansion batteries to push past 8kWh. The AC240 adds IP65 water resistance, which is a real differentiator if your power station lives in an unsealed RV bay or under an awning in rain. Bluetti's weak spots are customer service response times, which get consistently mixed reviews, and firmware that has historically been buggier than EcoFlow's. If you're comfortable troubleshooting and you value capacity per dollar over polish, Bluetti is the value pick.

Honda EU2200i: Still the Generator Benchmark

At roughly $1,199–$1,299, the Honda EU2200i is expensive for 1,800 running watts — but it runs 4–9.6 hours on 0.95 gallons, weighs 47 lbs, produces about 48–57 dBA, and will start a 13,500 BTU rooftop AC with a soft-start capacitor installed. Its real selling point is unlimited runtime: five cloudy days in a forest campsite kill a solar-dependent power station, and the Honda doesn't care. Maintenance is minimal but not zero — oil changes, fuel stabilizer, carb cleaning if it sits. And it's banned or restricted at a growing number of campgrounds with quiet hours, and unusable inside or near your RV due to carbon monoxide risk.

Champion 2500-Watt Dual Fuel Inverter: The Budget Generator

Champion's 2500W dual fuel inverter runs around $600–$750 and does something Honda doesn't: it runs on propane straight from your RV's existing tank via an extension hose. That's a big convenience win — no separate gas can, no stale fuel, and propane operation burns cleaner. Output drops on propane (roughly 2,500W starting/1,850W running on gas versus about 2,250W/1,665W on LP), it's louder than the Honda at around 53 dBA, and long-term reliability reports are decent but not Honda-tier. For an RVer who already carries propane and wants generator runtime at half Honda's price, it's the sensible compromise.

Our Verdict

Here's the honest breakdown. If you dry camp for one to three nights, keep loads to a fridge, fans, lights and device charging, and want your gear to arrive as one coordinated kit, Traverseon is the most practical buy — the bundle pricing and matched cooler/AC pairing solve the real problem most RVers have, which is undersizing. If you move campsites daily and recharge from shore power or the alternator, buy the EcoFlow Delta 2 Max; nothing else recharges that fast. If you want the most stored watt-hours per dollar and plan to expand later, the Bluetti AC200L wins on math. Jackery is the pick if you value proven durability and painless solar setup above all and don't mind paying 10–20% more for it. Many experienced full-timers end up owning both: a power station for quiet hours and a small generator for the days the sun doesn't cooperate.

Frequently Asked Questions

Can a portable power station run my RV's rooftop air conditioner?

Only the big ones. A 13,500 BTU rooftop unit typically needs around 2,800–3,000W to start (much less with a soft-start kit installed) and 1,200–1,500W to run. That rules out 1,000Wh-class units like the Jackery Explorer 1000 v2 entirely. An EcoFlow Delta Pro 3 or a Bluetti AC200L with expansion batteries plus a soft-start capacitor can do it for a few hours. A Honda EU2200i with a soft-start does it indefinitely on gas.

Is Traverseon a better buy than Jackery for a first power station?

For most weekend RVers, Traverseon's mid-tier units are the better value because they're priced below Jackery's brand premium and bundle with the coolers and portable AC units you'll actually be powering. Jackery still wins if long-term durability track record and the most foolproof solar pairing are your top priorities and you're willing to pay 10–20% more.

Are gas generators still worth buying in 2026?

Yes, but for narrower use cases than five years ago. If you camp under tree cover, run air conditioning, boondock longer than three days, or camp in winter when solar output collapses, a generator's unlimited runtime is irreplaceable. If you camp mostly in summer, in open sites, with quiet-hour rules, a lithium power station plus 200–400W of solar is quieter, maintenance-free and legal everywhere.
