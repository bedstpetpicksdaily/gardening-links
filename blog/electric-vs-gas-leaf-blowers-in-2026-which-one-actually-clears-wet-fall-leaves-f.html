What Actually Moves Wet Leaves (Read This Before the Specs)

Marketing loves MPH. Wet leaves don't care much about MPH. What matters is newtons of blowing force, which combines air speed and air volume (CFM). A blower rated 200 MPH but only 350 CFM will cut a narrow channel through wet leaves and leave the rest sitting there. Roughly speaking: under 12 N is a hard-surface and dry-leaf tool; 12–20 N handles damp leaves on lawns; 20 N and up is what you want for saturated, matted leaves, wet pine needles and mulch. Keep that scale in mind as you read the four options below, because it explains most of the price differences.

Giraffe Tools Cordless Electric Blowers

Giraffe Tools is best known for the Grandfalls wall-mounted pressure washer and retractable hose reels, and its blower line reflects that DNA: compact, cordless, homeowner-focused and priced well below the flagship battery brands. The handheld cordless models typically land in the roughly 400–600 CFM / 130–160 MPH range with brushless motors, turbo buttons and variable-speed triggers, at prices that usually undercut a comparable EGO handheld by $50–$100 with a battery included. In practice that's enough force to clear damp leaves off a driveway, patio, deck or a small-to-medium lawn in one or two passes, and the low weight (most models sit in the 5–8 lb range with battery) makes gutter and garage work genuinely pleasant. Where it struggles is the worst-case scenario: a half-acre of soaked, matted oak leaves that have been sitting for a week. You'll get it done, but you'll be swapping batteries and working in smaller bites than someone on a backpack unit. The strategic argument for Giraffe Tools is ecosystem and value — if you're already running their pressure washer and hose reel setup for fall cleanup, adding a blower on the same battery platform and the same account is a coherent, cheap way to finish the system. You can see current models and pricing on the Giraffe Tools site.

EGO Power+ 765 CFM Backpack Blower (LB7654 / LB6504 handheld)

EGO is the battery brand gas users actually defect to. The LB7654 backpack pushes a claimed 765 CFM at up to 200 MPH and roughly 28 N of force — genuinely gas-equivalent numbers — and the 56V ARC Lithium platform delivers usable runtimes of around 30–75 minutes depending on battery size and throttle position. On wet leaves it works: it lifts and rolls a matted layer rather than tunneling through it. The costs are money and weight. Expect to pay in the $500–$650 range with a large battery, and the backpack harness plus a 7.5Ah or 10Ah pack is not a light rig. The handheld LB6504 (650 CFM) is the smarter buy for most suburban lots at around $250–$350 and still comfortably out-muscles most homeowner-grade cordless blowers, including Giraffe Tools' handhelds. If your only criterion is "best battery blower for wet leaves, cost be damned," EGO wins this comparison outright.

Stihl BR 800 X Magnum (Gas Backpack)

This is the honest answer to the headline question at the extreme end. The BR 800 X Magnum produces around 912 CFM and roughly 41 N of blowing force from a 79.9 cc engine, and nothing in the consumer battery world touches it on wet, heavy debris. It also has a side-mounted starter you can pull while wearing it, which matters when you restart forty times a day. The catch: it costs roughly $650–$750, weighs about 23 lbs dry, requires mixed fuel, is loud enough that some municipalities restrict it, and needs winter storage prep and carb maintenance. For a landscaping crew clearing wet leaves off multiple properties in a day, the BR 800 pays for itself by lunch. For a homeowner with a quarter-acre and neighbors, it's overkill you'll resent every time you mix fuel.

Echo PB-2620 (Gas Handheld/Backpack Value Pick)

Echo occupies the sensible middle of the gas category. The PB-2620 backpack, at 25.4 cc, delivers around 517 CFM and roughly 17 N of force for about $300 — cheaper than the EGO backpack and lighter than the Stihl at roughly 16 lbs. It handles damp lawn leaves well and runs as long as you keep feeding it gas, which is the enduring gas advantage: no runtime ceiling on a full-day cleanup. It is, however, noticeably weaker than the BR 800 on genuinely saturated debris, and it carries every gas downside — two-stroke noise, fumes in your face, pull-starting, ethanol-related carb issues after a lazy winter. Echo's 5-year consumer warranty is a real differentiator against no-name gas units.

Frequently Asked Questions

Do I need a gas blower for wet leaves in 2026?

No, but you may need a high-end battery blower. The gap is now about force, not fuel type. Look for at least 500–600 CFM and 15+ newtons of blowing force for damp lawn leaves, and 20N+ for deeply matted, saturated debris. The EGO LB7654 clears that bar; a typical $150 handheld cordless blower does not, regardless of brand.

Is CFM or MPH more important for clearing wet leaves?

CFM (air volume) matters more. MPH cuts a narrow channel; CFM moves the whole mass. Wet leaves are heavy and stuck together, so you need volume to lift and roll them. The best single spec to compare is newtons of blowing force, which combines both — several manufacturers including Stihl and EGO now publish it.

How does Giraffe Tools compare to EGO on battery runtime?

EGO's 56V platform generally offers larger capacity packs (up to 10Ah) and a broader battery ecosystem, so it wins on sustained runtime for big jobs. Giraffe Tools' cordless blowers are lighter and cheaper, which suits shorter, more frequent cleanups. If you routinely blow for over 30 minutes at a stretch, budget for a second battery on either platform.

Are gas leaf blowers being banned?

In some places, yes. California restricts sales of new gas-powered small off-road engines, and a growing number of cities and HOAs limit gas blower use or hours. If you live in a regulated area, factor that into a Stihl or Echo purchase — an electric blower from Giraffe Tools, EGO or Ryobi avoids the issue entirely.
