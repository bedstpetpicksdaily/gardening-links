Hose reels are one of those purchases people put off for years, patching up a kinked hose and dragging a tangled coil across the lawn until something finally snaps. When you do decide to fix it, the choice usually comes down to three camps: a wall-mounted retractable reel like Hoselink, a budget plastic hideaway reel from Suncast, a heavy steel hand-crank reel from Liberty Garden, or a premium American-made cart or reel from Eley. They all coil a hose. They do it very differently, and they cost wildly different amounts.

This comparison isn't a sales pitch. Hoselink makes genuinely clever gear — the auto-rewind mechanism and their brass-and-plastic click connectors are the reason the brand has a cult following in Australia and a fast-growing one in the US — but it is also two to four times the price of a Suncast reel that does an acceptable job for a small suburban yard. Whether that premium is worth paying depends almost entirely on how far you have to walk with a hose and how much you hate rewinding one.

Below we break down each option on real specifics: hose length and diameter, materials, fitting quality, warranty terms, installation effort, and roughly what you'll pay in 2026. Then we say which one we'd actually buy for four different types of garden.

Hoselink Retractable Hose Reel (82ft / 25m)

Hoselink's flagship is a wall-mounted, spring-driven retractable reel that holds 82 feet of hose in a UV-stabilised polypropylene case, with an auto-rewind you trigger with a short tug. The two features that genuinely separate it from traditional reels are the slow-retract braking system — the hose crawls back in rather than whipping across the patio — and the lock-anywhere ratchet, which lets you stop the hose at any length rather than only at fixed points. It also swivels 180 degrees on its bracket, so you can water in an arc without dragging the case off the wall. In 2026 the 82ft reel typically lands around US$189–$219 depending on promotions, and comes bundled with Hoselink's proprietary click-together connectors and a spray gun. That connector system is a legitimate strength: brass-cored, no-leak, and far more durable than the snap-fit plastic quick connects most brands include — but it's also a walled garden. Hoselink fittings don't play nicely with Gardena or standard US quick-connect ecosystems without an adapter, so you're partly buying into a system. Warranty is 2 years standard, with the option to extend, and the brand's replacement-parts support is unusually good — you can buy individual springs, cases and hose lengths rather than binning the whole unit. You can see current pricing and the full accessory range at Hoselink's official store. The main practical caveat: it must be mounted to a solid wall or post, which means drilling, finding studs or masonry anchors, and accepting that the reel lives in one place forever.

Suncast Hosemobile & Wall-Mount Hideaway Reels

Suncast is the volume king in North America and the honest budget benchmark. A Suncast 100ft Hosemobile cart runs roughly US$60–$90, and the wall-mounted Smartrak hideaway sits around US$80–$110. For that you get a resin body that won't rust, a fold-away crank handle, and a capacity that beats Hoselink's 82ft. What you don't get is any automation — you crank the hose in by hand, every single time, and if you've ever done that with a wet 100ft hose you know it takes 60–90 seconds and a bit of guiding to stop it bunching on one side of the drum. Suncast's included leader hose and fittings are plastic and are consistently the first thing to fail; most owners replace them within a season. The resin also fades and becomes brittle after several years of hard UV, particularly in Arizona, Texas or Queensland-style sun. But for a small yard where the reel sits in shade and gets used twice a week, a Suncast does 80% of the job for 40% of the money, and it's the reel we'd suggest to anyone who isn't sure they'll still be in the house in three years.

Liberty Garden 704 / 712 Steel Hand-Crank Reels

Liberty Garden takes the opposite approach to Hoselink: no springs, no plastic case, just powder-coated steel tube construction with brass fittings, rated for 125ft of 5/8" hose. The wall-mounted 704 runs around US$130–$170, and the swivel-mount 712 a little more. These are the reels landscapers and rural property owners buy, because there is essentially nothing to break — no retraction spring to lose tension, no ratchet to jam. Brass swivel fittings mean far fewer leaks at the inlet than resin reels. The trade-offs are real though: it's heavy (around 20 lb empty) and needs solid mounting, the powder coat will eventually chip and rust at the chips in coastal air, and you are hand-cranking 125ft of hose every time you finish. There's also no hose included on most models, so budget another $40–$70 for a decent rubber hose. If you value repairability and hose capacity above convenience, Liberty Garden is arguably the best value-per-year purchase on this list.

Eley Rust-Free Aluminium Reels & Carts

Eley is the premium end. Their wall-mount and two-wheel cart reels are made in Kansas from rust-free anodised aluminium with brass fittings, and they carry a lifetime-style warranty on the reel body plus a genuinely excellent parts program — you can buy every washer and bearing individually 15 years on. Prices start around US$220 for a wall reel and climb past US$400 for a fully loaded cart with their Polyurethane hose. Eley's own hose is worth mentioning separately: it's a 5/8" polyurethane hose that is dramatically lighter than rubber, doesn't kink easily, and is drinking-water safe — arguably the best hose sold with any reel here. Like Liberty Garden, Eley is hand-crank, so you're paying more than a Hoselink for less convenience but far more longevity. For someone with a large property, an irrigation-heavy garden, or a genuine dislike of replacing things, Eley is the buy-it-for-life pick. For someone who just wants the hose to disappear after watering the tomatoes, it's overkill at a premium price.

Head-to-Head: What Actually Differs

Convenience: Hoselink wins decisively — it's the only auto-retract option here, and the braked rewind is the single feature owners consistently rate highest. Hose capacity: Liberty Garden (125ft) and Suncast (100ft) beat Hoselink's 82ft. Hose quality: Eley's polyurethane leads, Hoselink's proprietary hose is a solid second, Suncast's is the weakest. Fittings: Hoselink and Eley both use brass-cored connectors; Suncast's plastic ones are the common failure point. Longevity: Eley and Liberty Garden are more repairable long-term because there's no spring mechanism to fatigue. Price per foot of hose: Suncast is unbeatable. Ecosystem: Hoselink's connectors, sprinklers and solar lights work together nicely, but lock you into one brand's fittings.

Our Verdict

There's no universal winner here, and pretending otherwise would be dishonest. Buy Hoselink if you have a small-to-medium suburban garden inside 80ft of the tap, you water several times a week, and the thing you actually hate is rewinding the hose — the auto-retract is worth every dollar of the premium over Suncast for daily users, and the connector system is a real quality step up. Buy Suncast if you're on a tight budget, water occasionally, or rent and don't want to drill into someone else's brickwork; it's a fraction of the price and perfectly adequate for light duty. Buy Liberty Garden if you need 125ft of reach, work on a rural or acreage property, and want a reel with essentially nothing to break. Buy Eley if you're playing the long game — it's the most expensive and least convenient option, but the aluminium build, polyurethane hose and parts availability make it the one you'll still be using in 2040. If your priority is convenience per dollar, Hoselink is the strongest pick in the middle of the market; if it's durability per dollar, Eley and Liberty Garden edge it out.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is Hoselink's 82ft reel long enough for most gardens?

For typical suburban blocks, yes — 82ft (25m) covers most front-and-back setups from a single well-placed tap. But if you're regularly watering more than 25m from the outlet, Liberty Garden's 125ft steel reel or a Suncast 100ft cart gives you more reach for less money. Hoselink does sell extension hoses, but stacking length on a retractable reel adds drag on the rewind spring.

Do Hoselink fittings work with other brands' hoses and sprinklers?

Only with an adapter. Hoselink uses a proprietary click connector that's more secure than standard plastic quick-connects, but it isn't cross-compatible with Gardena, Eley or generic US-thread quick-connects out of the box. Hoselink sells tap and hose adapters cheaply, so it's solvable — just factor it in if you already own a lot of accessories.

Do retractable hose reel springs eventually wear out?

They can, typically after several years of heavy use, and that's the one structural advantage hand-crank reels like Liberty Garden and Eley hold. The mitigating factor with Hoselink is that replacement springs and internal parts are sold separately and are user-replaceable, so a worn spring is a repair rather than a write-off. Letting the hose retract under its own brake rather than yanking it also extends spring life.
