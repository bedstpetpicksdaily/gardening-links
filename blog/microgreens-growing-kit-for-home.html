Why Everyone's Buying This Now

1. Fall is the natural "indoor pivot" moment

Once nighttime temps drop below 45°F, most outdoor edibles stall out. Microgreens don't care — they germinate and finish in a temperature range most homes already sit in (65–75°F). Gardeners who just pulled their tomato plants are buying trays now specifically so they don't lose their growing rhythm through winter. Buying in early fall also means you've dialed in your process before the holidays, when fresh garnishes and salads actually matter.

2. Fresh greens are expensive and spoil fast

A clamshell of specialty micro-arugula or pea shoots regularly runs $4–$8 and wilts in four days. A single ounce of broccoli or radish seed — usually $3–$6 — fills a 10x20 tray and yields a comparable harvest, repeatedly. Home growers are doing the math and it favors the tray, especially if you eat greens more than twice a week.

3. It's the lowest-risk way to garden indoors

Unlike indoor herbs or dwarf citrus, microgreens don't need to mature, flower, or fruit. There's no pest cycle to manage, no repotting, no pruning. You harvest before most problems have time to appear. For anyone who has killed a windowsill basil plant, the 10-day cycle is forgiving — a failed tray costs you a dollar of seed and ten days, not a season.

What to Look For

  • A true drainage + reservoir tray pair. The single most important feature is a set of nested trays: a perforated top tray for the growing medium and a solid bottom tray to hold water. Bottom-watering is what prevents mold and damping-off, which is the #1 reason beginner trays fail. Kits with only one solid tray force you to water from above — avoid them.
  • Included grow light (or a plan for one). From October through February, a north- or east-facing window won't give you enough light, and you'll get pale, leggy, stretched greens. Look for a kit with a full-spectrum LED bar of at least 15–20 watts positioned 6–12 inches above the canopy, or budget separately for one. This is the difference between crisp, colorful microgreens and sad yellow threads.
  • Seed quantity and variety — check the actual weight. Many kits advertise "6 varieties" but include sample packets of a few grams each, enough for one thin tray. Look for at least 1 ounce per variety, and prioritize proven fast growers: broccoli, radish, pea shoots, sunflower, and mustard. Confirm the seeds are sold for sprouting/microgreens use.
  • Tray dimensions that fit your space. Standard 10x20 trays are the commercial norm and give the biggest harvest, but they need real counter space. If you're working with a small kitchen, look for 5x5 or 5x10 trays — several smaller trays also let you stagger plantings so you harvest something every few days instead of all at once.

Frequently Asked Questions

Do I really need a grow light, or will a sunny window work in winter?

In summer, a bright south-facing window is usually enough. From late October through February at northern latitudes, it generally isn't — you'll get leggy, pale growth that tastes bland and flops over. Since you're buying specifically for the indoor season, get a kit with a light included or add a basic full-spectrum LED bar. Run it 12–16 hours a day starting once the seeds have sprouted and you've removed the blackout cover.

How long from seed to harvest, and how many harvests per tray?

Most varieties finish in 7–14 days: radish and mustard on the fast end (6–8 days), broccoli and kale around 8–12, cilantro and beet on the slow end (14–21). Microgreens are a one-cut crop — you harvest at the base with scissors and then compost the root mat and start fresh. Pea shoots are the exception and will sometimes give you a smaller second cut. Plan on composting and reseeding each tray, and stagger two or three trays a few days apart for a continuous supply.

Why did my tray get white fuzzy mold, and can I still eat it?

Most "mold" on day 2–4 is actually root hairs — fine white fuzz clustered right at the root, which disappears when misted. True mold is spidery, spreads across the surface and seed hulls, and smells sour. It's caused by three things: seeding too densely, watering from the top, and no airflow. Fix it by sowing a thinner layer, watering only into the bottom reservoir tray, and running a small fan nearby for a few hours a day. If it's genuinely mold, compost the tray and start over — it's a $2 loss, not worth the risk.

Frequently Asked Questions

How long does it take to grow microgreens from a kit?

Most microgreens are ready to harvest in 7-14 days from planting, depending on the variety and growing conditions. Faster-growing varieties like radish and mustard can be harvested in as little as 5-7 days, while slower varieties like broccoli may take 12-14 days. Once harvested, you can replant immediately for continuous production.

Do microgreens kits need sunlight or grow lights?

Most microgreens kits require either 6+ hours of indirect natural sunlight daily or grow lights placed 3-6 inches above the seedlings. LED grow lights are ideal for indoor kits as they produce minimal heat and use less electricity than traditional options. Without adequate light, microgreens will become leggy and weak.

What's the difference between microgreens and sprouts from a kit?

Microgreens are grown in soil and harvested after developing their first true leaves (7-14 days), while sprouts are grown in water and eaten with the whole root intact (3-5 days). Microgreens have more flavor, nutrition, and shelf life than sprouts, but sprouts require less equipment and grow faster. Choose a microgreens kit if you want more versatile produce for cooking.

Can I reuse the soil in a microgreens growing kit?

You can reuse soil once or twice, but it's not recommended for best results since nutrients deplete after the first harvest and disease risk increases. Most growers replace the soil completely after 2-3 harvests to maintain healthy, vigorous growth and prevent mold or pathogen buildup. Fresh soil is inexpensive and ensures superior microgreens quality.
